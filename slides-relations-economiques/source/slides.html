01 · Introduction

Definition and main forms

International economic relations are all the economic exchanges between countries: movements of goods, services, capital, labor and technology across borders. They shape growth, employment, prices and the development level of every economy.

International trade

The exchange of goods and services between countries.

e.g., Algeria’s gas exports to Europe

International investment

Capital invested abroad, especially FDI: a firm acquires or builds productive assets in another country.

e.g., a foreign carmaker opening a factory

International financial flows

Movements of money between countries.

Loans, migrants’ remittances, portfolio investment, currency exchange
Three main forms

02 · Classical theories

Mercantilism

The dominant economic doctrine in Europe from the 16th to the 18th century. A nation’s wealth is measured by its stock of precious metals (gold and silver), and the state must actively manage trade to accumulate them.

Zero-sum game What one country gains, another necessarily loses.
Main principles
  • Export as much as possible, import as little as possible, to build a trade surplus
  • Strong state intervention in the economy
  • Protection of domestic industries
  • Colonies as sources of raw materials and markets
Tools used
TariffsImport bansExport subsidiesTrade monopolies
Limit
It confuses money with real wealth. A country can hold a lot of gold and still produce little.

02 · Classical theories

Adam Smith and absolute advantage

Smith criticizes mercantilism: a nation’s wealth lies in its production capacity (labor, productivity), not in its stock of gold. Free trade and the division of labor increase this wealth.

Definition

A country has an absolute advantage in a good when it can produce it using fewer resources (less labor) than another country.

1Specialize in the goods it produces most efficiently
2Import the goods others produce more efficiently
3Trade is a positive-sum game: all countries can gain
Limit: the theory does not explain trade for a country that has no absolute advantage in any good.

02 · Classical theories

David Ricardo and comparative advantage

David Ricardo
Principles of Political Economy and Taxation · 1817

Trade can benefit both countries even if one is more efficient in everything.

Definition

A country has a comparative advantage in a good when its opportunity cost of producing it (the quantity of another good it must give up) is lower than in the other country.

03 · Modern theories

Heckscher-Ohlin theory

Eli Heckscher · 1919Bertil Ohlin · 1933

Unlike Ricardo (differences in technology), it explains trade by differences in factor endowments: the relative quantity of production factors a country possesses (labor, capital and land).

Principle

A country exports the goods that use intensively its abundant factor, and imports the goods that require its scarce factor.

Labor-abundant · e.g., Bangladesh
exports labor-intensive goods: textiles
Capital-abundant · e.g., Germany
exports capital-intensive goods: machinery
Criticism · the Leontief paradox (1953)
Leontief tested the theory on the United States, the most capital-abundant country: its exports were more labor-intensive than its imports. This pushed economists to look for other explanations.

03 · Modern theories

New Trade Theory

Paul Krugman · late 1970s – early 1980sNobel Prize 2008

It responds to a fact earlier theories could not explain: most trade takes place between similar countries, in similar goods.

Economies of scale

The more a firm produces, the lower its average cost per unit. Production concentrates in a few places and serves the world market.

Imperfect competition

Markets are dominated by a few large firms, not by many small identical ones.

Product variety

Consumers value choice, so countries exchange differentiated versions of the same product (intra-industry trade).

Policy implication Because of scale effects and first-mover advantages, state support for strategic industries can sometimes be justified.

04 · Limitations

Inequalities between countries

The theories conclude that trade benefits everyone, but in practice the gains are unequally shared.

01

Many developing countries depend on exporting raw materials with volatile prices (e.g., Algeria and hydrocarbons), while developed countries export higher-value manufactured goods.

02

This creates dependence and vulnerability to external shocks such as price drops.

03

Within countries, trade creates winners and losers: consumers and exporters gain, workers in industries exposed to import competition may lose their jobs.

04

These observations feed the debate between free trade and protectionism, and critical approaches such as dependency theory.

04 · Contemporary relevance

Global value chains and multinational corporations

A global value chain (GVC) is the splitting of the production process of a good across several countries, each specializing in a stage.

Research & designHigh value
Components
Assembly
MarketingHigh value
Services
Consequence Countries no longer trade only finished goods, they trade tasks.
Multinational corporations

Firms that operate in several countries. They organize these chains and move capital, technology and know-how through FDI.

Advantages
  • Lower production costs
  • Technology transfer and job creation in host countries
Risks
  • Dependence on a few suppliers or regions
  • Disruptions (e.g., supply chain crisis of 2020-2021)
  • Unequal value: design and branding stay mostly in developed countries

Traditional theories, built for trade in finished goods between nations, only partly explain this reality.

05 · Conclusion

Theories of international trade evolved alongside reality

16th–18th c.
Mercantilism

Trade as a zero-sum game, wealth measured in gold

1776 · 1817
Smith & Ricardo

Specialization and comparative advantage, with gains for all

1919 · 1933
Heckscher-Ohlin

Trade explained by factor endowments

Late 1970s
Krugman

Economies of scale, imperfect competition and variety

Each theory corrects the limits of the previous one, yet none explains everything. Today, with global value chains, persistent inequalities and a mix of open trade and protectionist measures, the debate remains open.

Answer to the problématique

Theories explain why countries trade, but they must be combined with the realities of the global economy to understand it fully.
